The motion

Why it’s a cracker

“This House Believes The Algorithm Will Become More Authoritative Than The ‘Ālim”

“Authoritative”

Trusted? Obeyed? Consulted first? Legitimate? Each side will want its own definition, so listen for it in the first speeches.

“Will become”

A prediction about the future, not a verdict on what should happen. Is the side arguing is, will or ought?

“The ‘Ālim”

The scholar as a person: learning, character, accountability, a chain of teachers. Authority for whom, and in what domain?

Proposition

Must show that, on balance, the algorithm will come to carry more authority than the scholar.

Opposition

Must show that it won’t: that the scholar’s authority will hold or stay greater.

Line to borrowA motion about who we trust, chaired by an Australian in South Africa. What could possibly go wrong?

Introducing speakers

Short, warm, identical in length

  • Same length for everyone. Two lines each; neutrality lives in the details.
  • Name, role, one human detail. Skip the full CV.
  • Say the side and slot. “Opening for the Proposition…”
  • Start the clock on their first word, not when they stand up.
  • Thank each speaker by name as they sit. Same warmth both sides.
Template line “Opening for the [Proposition / Opposition], please welcome [Name], [role], who [one line]. [First name], your five minutes start when you do.”

Floor contributions

One minute, many voices

Picking fairly

  • Take two or three hands at a time, then let them speak in turn.
  • Balance women and men, front and back, left and right of the room.
  • Alternate leaning-for and leaning-against where you can tell.
  • Newcomers first: favour people who haven’t yet spoken.
  • Ask each to give their name and keep to one point.

Illustrative numbers, not a prediction. Side A still has the most votes, but Side B wins: it moved more.

  • Agree the method with organisers in advance (hands, cards, app) and whether “undecided” is an option.
  • Use percentages if people arrive or leave mid-debate.
  • Two counters, one per side of the room. Write the numbers down.
  • Announce in order: before → after → change → winner.
“Before the debate: [x] for, [y] against. Now: [x] for, [y] against. The side that moved the House furthest, and wins tonight’s debate, is… the [side].”
General chairing tips & tricks

Ten for the pocket

  1. Be the most relaxed person in the room. Calm is contagious.
  2. Name the stakes early: why this motion matters to this room.
  3. Keep intros short. The speakers are the show.
  4. One-line bridge between speeches: who spoke, who’s next. No commentary.
  5. Read the room. Flagging energy? A smile and a brisk pace fix most things.
  6. Equal everything: intro length, grace time, warmth, eye contact.
  7. Have a backup if a speaker is missing or the tech fails (see contingencies).
  8. Use names, never “the last speaker”.
  9. Pause before announcing. Let the moment land.
  10. Enjoy it. Chairs who enjoy themselves make debates people remember.
Umpire’s creedThe best umpires are the ones nobody talks about at the end.

Contingencies

If it all goes pear-shaped

Tech fails

Mic dies? Speaker steps forward, the room leans in. Stop the clock for genuine faults only. Keep a paper backup for the vote.

Speaker absent

Agree in advance: a teammate takes the slot, or the side runs with two (the order still alternates). Announce it plainly, no fuss.

Time runs short

Trim the floor, never a speech. Shrink to 30-second contributions, then go straight to the vote.

A tie

Equal movement? Agree the tie-break with organisers beforehand. If none: “The House has spoken, and it spoke evenly.” A draw is a result.

Heated exchange

Stand. Pause. “Let’s take a breath; strong views, and good manners.” Then return to the speaker on the clock.

Your secret weapon

Silence. A calm chair who waits a beat is more powerful than any gavel.

Closing remarks · word for word

Friends, before we part, a word about what just happened in this room.

For fifty minutes, six people stood up, made a case, were challenged, and answered. And you weighed them, changed your minds or held your ground, and said so openly. That is one of the oldest ways we have of seeking the truth.

Our tradition carried knowledge hand to hand: teacher to student, link by link, each one a person who could be questioned and who answered for what they taught. Tonight the Proposition asked us to take seriously how fast the world is changing. The Opposition asked us to take seriously what must endure. Both are acts of care.

We will build extraordinary tools. May we have the wisdom to remain their masters, and the humility to remember what we ourselves do not know.

And may we remember that the most important thing here tonight was never the motion. It was us: a community that argues with courtesy, listens with generosity, and leaves as friends.

Whichever way you voted, thank you. That’s stumps.
